# my first work experience in finance

Throughout the past 4 months I've worked at British Columbia Investment Management Corporation (BCI) as an analyst. Looking back, I have a few main takeaways that I'd like to share.

## What I accomplished

- Implemented a factor risk model to explore thematic factor research for the global active equities portfolios
- Built out a credit model that quantifies climate risk in different NGFS climate change scenarios
- Automated and migrated excel financial models and VBA code to python scripts.

## Deriving Value by Adding Value

I've never enjoyed the idea of a full-time job, much less one that I wasn't "passionate" about. I think the whole "follow your passion" way of thinking is extremely overrated and can be damaging depending on the person.

The main reason I believe this is that most people don't actually have a passion to follow. I personally have struggled with the feeling that I wasn't passionate about anything specific (or anything career-worthy). This work experience helped me realize that, while some people have an underlying passion from which they derive meaning and value, I (and I would argue, most people) don't have a linear path they need to commit to. I personally find that a liberating conclusion, and I think it detaches me from the idea of pursuing prestige for the sake of prestige, or locking in a decision too early.

That's where I think adding value comes in. When you're not chasing a passion, what you're left with is a simpler question: what can I actually contribute here? I've found that's a much more useful thing to optimize for.

## Autonomy and Culture

I learned in my 2A term about some key factors that play a role in workplace satisfaction and motivation. The two that always stood out to me were autonomy and culture. I'd always been under the impression that compensation was the best motivator, and that my organizational behaviour professor was talking nonsense. That was until I started working here.

When I decompose why I enjoyed my time at BCI, it really comes down to a few key factors, none of them being the pay. For one, the company culture is extremely welcoming and inclusive. My coworkers were very easy to be around, and as someone who struggles meeting new people, I always felt comfortable asking questions or getting clarification. This extends to things like dress code, where the "dress for your day" policy made me feel like I had more control over my experience at work. While that's just one example, I think my experience as a whole can be summed up by two things: the culture, and the autonomy that came with it.

## Distance and Velocity

This opportunity required me to move to Victoria, British Columbia for 4 months. It was my first time being away from anyone I knew for more than a day. To say I was nervous going in is an understatement. In hindsight, I'm not sure what I was so worried about, though there were definitely some hard parts.

Without getting too far into the details, I learned the importance of keeping yourself sane. Whether that's going out for walks, writing your thoughts down, or just having a really good meal (the food in Victoria is amazing). Living alone with limited social interaction outside of work is tough.

---

I'm walking away from this term with a lot more than I expected. In addition to technical skills and a line on a resume, I have a clearer sense of what I actually want out of work and life in general. I came in nervous and uncertain about pretty much everything, and I'm leaving with more confidence in my ability to figure things out as I go. That's probably the most valuable thing I took from the last four months.
