# inspiration

last summer i found a love for the game of catan. i really enjoyed the dynamics of the game and how the decision making and information management can influence strategy. with these kinds of games, i do tend to always ask myself: "what's the most optimal way that i can improve my expertise in the game?" my thought process took me to games like chess or poker, where there are either tools for training decision making, or methods for evaluating skill in-game. i researched potential tools that already exist to hopefully find a resource that i can use, only to find that none exist. so, naturally, i decided to take it on myself.

## approximating perfect play

one common dimension of poker and chess is that they have some form of perfect play. for poker, gto is the standard assessment of what "good" is, and for chess it's stockfish. by complexity, it's pretty trivial to determine that catan more-so fits the archetype of chess, and thus i decided to make stockfish for catan.

# terminology

- win-share: % of games won in a set of games played. even win-share equates to 25% in 4p games, and win-share can also be expressed as above/below even (e.g., a win-share of 35% is 15% above even)
- vs-field: games where one individual (can be a bot or a human) plays against three identical players
- field share: an individual's win-share in a vs-field
- when i say a version had a field share of x% against an older one, the newer version is the one individual and three copies of the older version make up the field

# the first approach

i wanted to approach this open problem with an incremental approach. i first started with a heuristic engine, which scores every legal move with a hand-tuned evaluation and plays the best one. it weighs points, production and its variety, room to expand, road length, the leader's production, and hand size against the discard limit. over time i also implemented development cards and largest army. another key focus was the idea of information fairness: a bot should only be able to see what a human can also see in the same spot. when it needs to imagine the hidden cards, it samples them from what it believes.

this approach was actually not terrible for what it was, but i still needed more. i decided to implement a search algorithm.

# looking ahead

the first search algorithm involved shortlisting the best moves using the heuristic engine as an evaluation tool. for each of the best moves, the search imagines the hidden cards, plays the game forward with heuristic players, and keeps the move that wins most often. after testing the new iteration in a vs-field, results showed that the search engine had a field share of 47.8% against the heuristic engines.

from there i made some smaller fixes. for example, ending your turn was played out one turn further than any other move, so ending your turn often had positive bias. another early issue showed that short look-aheads made close-to-random choices, which was fixed using sequential halving: the search splits its budget into rounds, plays out every remaining move each round, and drops the weaker half until one move is left. after making these changes the new search was 3x faster and had a field share of 35.8% against the older version of the search engine.

i was approaching the limits of what hard-coded engines could accomplish, and while i was happy with initial results, i really wanted to see how far i could take this.

# a learned judge

one part of stockfish that i really loved during my days playing chess was the evaluation bar. i also thought that it had a good use in my next iteration of the engine. the idea was a small network that could predict who wins from one player's view. the inputs would be points, production, cards, and its beliefs about opponent hands. the search would then stop looking ahead after 8 turns and ask the network who's winning. i called this iteration value-1.

the network was trained on a few positions sampled from each game, so that it can't memorize whole games and overfit. after training the network on many, many games, the results were pretty solid: value-1 had a field share of 31% against the previous iteration.

# trials and tribulations

after this point i attempted a variety of new ideas that could potentially make the engine even better:

1. a policy network to pick which moves to search: field share of 21.5%, just below even, which meant that reordering the shortlist made the engine slightly worse rather than better (policy networks come back later, i promise)
2. a search network with no look-ahead: the network would judge the position right after the move. this idea was abysmal, giving a field share of 3.3%

i also randomly decided to test simulating about 4x as many positions. this gave a field share of 29.8%, which indicated that the current search engine's main weakness was probably noise.

# self-iteration

value-1 was trained on games that heuristic engines played, which meant that it only really knew how those types of games ended, not how search engine games ended. value-2 ended up being the same framework as value-1, with the core difference being the training data used. value-2's network was trained on 32,000 search games instead of heuristic games.

the results were pretty interesting this time around. on held-out games value-2 was actually not shown to predict better than value-1, but still played stronger against the search engine in vs-field, generating a field share of 30%. my best guess is that value-2 learned from the kind of games it actually plays, so it judged the positions the search hands it better, even if it wasn't more accurate overall. i also decided to implement mixed opponents so that the engine doesn't pick up habits only its own copies have: an anti-leader player, a road rusher, an eager trader, and a stingy one. this went on to become the value-3 engine.

the trajectory i was on was very promising, with one major issue: the engines so far had no training on trades.

# trading

the general approach was pretty simple: judge a trade by how much it changes each side's chance of winning. turn down trades that help the partner as much as you, or ones that help the leader. the problem with letting the search engine trade freely is that its look-ahead players never trade, so it couldn't value trades properly: it made offers it didn't actually want, and backed out of around 60% of its own offers after other players accepted them.

the fix was to check the rule's offers and answers by playing each one out against not trading, in the same world. in practice the field share against searches using the new rule alone was 49.2%. from this point every strength test ran with trading on, and i developed the current iteration of the engine: basalt-1. human testers win around 12% of their games when they play against three basalt-1s.

# basalt-2

at this point i spent a good amount of time thinking about the architecture of the engine. the search framework, while strong, had a significant flaw: the search covers for the network. if you recall, the search only asks the network after 8 simulated turns, and those turns are played by heuristic players. any bad moves they make along the way are already baked into the position by the time the network sees it, so the network ends up judging the heuristic players' mistakes instead of the move the search was testing. the fix was to change the way the network judges the board.

here are the four main changes that basalt-2 will have:

1. board vision: add board summaries, such as how many roads to the nearest good spot, the best spots within reach, spots an opponent can reach first, and whether it's boxed in
2. better targets: learn from the search engine's own estimate of each position, which is far less noisy than one winner per game
3. the right positions: train on the positions the search actually asks about, including mid-turn ones
4. a policy head that can bring in moves outside of the heuristic's shortlist

overall i'm excited to see how these changes do. the test will be the same as always: basalt-2's field share against basalt-1, and how often testers win against it.
